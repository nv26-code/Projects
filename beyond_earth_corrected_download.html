Chapter one · Moon

The Moon keeps clues

On the Moon, we can find footprints near tools left by Apollo astronauts. Among the tools, there were some that listened for moonquakes; others checked the surface. The powered experiments stopped long ago, but special reflectors still let researchers measure the Moon from Earth.

Apollo science instruments · What was inside the Moon?Inactive

Apollo astronauts placed science instruments on the lunar surface, including seismometers that detected moonquakes. Together with returned rock samples, their measurements helped scientists study lunar geology—the Moon’s rocks and history—and its layered interior. The surface instrument packages are no longer operating.

Laser-ranging retroreflectors · A useful bounceStill useful for measurements · passive

Apollo astronauts left arrays of tiny reflectors that bounce laser light back toward Earth. By timing the light’s round trip, scientists measure the Moon’s distance and how its motion changes. The reflectors need no batteries or radio; some are still used in laser-ranging research.

Chapter two · Mars

A toolbox on the red planet

A lander touches down and works from one spot. A rover drives across the ground to investigate more places. An orbiter circles a world from above. On Mars, each kind of tool asks a different question: What is the weather like? What do the rocks remember? Could water—and conditions suitable for life—once have existed here?

Viking 1 and 2 · First long-working Mars laboratoriesInactive

The Viking landers arrived in 1976. Their cameras, weather tools, and soil experiments studied the surface and searched for signs of life; the biology results did not establish that life was present. Viking also showed why planetary protection matters: spacecraft must be cleaned carefully so Earth microbes do not confuse the search for life elsewhere.

Sojourner · A small rover, a big testInactive

In 1997, Sojourner showed that a wheeled robot could explore Mars. It examined nearby rocks and soil while the Pathfinder lander helped send its findings home. Its mission ended when contact with Pathfinder was lost.

Spirit · Signs of a wetter pastInactive

Spirit explored Mars from 2004 and found evidence that water had changed some rocks and soil long ago, including silica-rich deposits. It far outlasted its planned mission but is no longer communicating.

Opportunity · Reading water in rocksInactive

Opportunity explored Mars from 2004 until NASA declared its mission over in 2019, after its last communication in June 2018. Minerals and rock layers it studied provided strong evidence that liquid water once shaped parts of Mars. It traveled and worked far beyond its planned mission.

Curiosity · Could ancient Mars have supported life?Still operating

Curiosity landed in 2012. Its cameras and rock-testing instruments study Gale Crater. It found evidence that an ancient lake environment had ingredients and conditions that could have supported microbial life. That is not the same as finding life.

Perseverance · Saving clues for laterStill operating

Perseverance landed in 2021 at Jezero Crater, a place with evidence of an ancient river delta. It studies rocks and collects carefully sealed samples for possible future return to Earth. Its work asks whether this area once had habitable conditions and looks for possible signs of ancient microbial life.

Ingenuity · Flying on another worldInactive

Ingenuity arrived with Perseverance and made the first powered, controlled flights on another planet in 2021. Built as a technology demonstration, it greatly exceeded its planned test flights. Its flying mission ended in 2024 after rotor damage.

Chapter three · Deep space

Messages across the dark

After a flyby, a probe can keep traveling: nothing makes it stop when its planned observations end. Its radio signal can cross enormous distances to antennas on Earth.

Voyager 1 and 2 · The outer solar systemStill communicating · capabilities change

Launched in 1977, both Voyagers studied Jupiter and Saturn. Voyager 2 also visited Uranus and Neptune, sending close-up measurements and images that transformed our understanding of their atmospheres, rings, and moons. Their planned planetary encounters ended, but both continued outward and have communicated from interstellar space. Teams may switch off instruments as power declines; check NASA for current status.

New Horizons · Pluto and beyondExtended mission · operating

New Horizons flew past Pluto in 2015 and the Kuiper Belt object Arrokoth in 2019. Its images and measurements revealed complex icy landscapes and helped scientists investigate distant small worlds. In June 2026, it safely awakened from a 321-day hibernation, and NASA reported that it was preparing to transmit stored science data from the distant Kuiper Belt.

A distant message is faint and takes time to arrive. NASA’s large ground antennas listen for the radio signal, and scientists turn the received data into pictures, graphs, and new questions.

A timeline of tools and discoveries

These dates mark milestones, not the moment every machine stopped working. Use the status key to distinguish completed missions from ongoing ones.

  1. 1960s · Apollo exploration begins. Apollo 11 astronauts place a laser retroreflector on the Moon in 1969. Later Apollo missions, including Apollo 14 and Apollo 15, add more reflectors and scientific instruments.
  2. 1970s · Mars and outer-planet exploration. Viking 1 and 2 land on Mars in 1976 to study its weather, soil and possible signs of life. Voyager 1 and 2 launch in 1977 to investigate the outer planets.
  3. 1997 · Sojourner explores Mars. The first successful Mars rover demonstrates how a small mobile robot can explore another planet. Its mission is now complete.
  4. 2004–2011 · Spirit and Opportunity explore Mars. The twin rovers investigate Martian rocks and search for evidence of past water. Spirit's mission ends in 2011, while Opportunity continues until 2019.
  5. 2006–2019 · New Horizons explores distant worlds. New Horizons launches in 2006, flies past Pluto in 2015 and completes its flyby of Arrokoth in 2019.
  6. 2012–2024 · Modern Mars exploration advances. Curiosity begins exploring Mars in 2012. Perseverance lands in 2021 and begins collecting rock and soil samples. Ingenuity demonstrates powered flight on another planet, completing 72 flights before its flying mission ends in January 2024.
  7. Ongoing · Exploration continues. Lunar laser retroreflectors remain useful for scientific measurements. Curiosity and Perseverance continue exploring Mars. Voyager 1, Voyager 2 and New Horizons continue their journeys through distant space, with their communication and scientific instrument activity subject to change.

How do we know?

Scientists compare data from instruments, study spacecraft images, examine samples such as Apollo Moon rocks, and interpret signals received on Earth. Several independent clues together provide stronger evidence than a single picture alone. A machine can finish its mission while the evidence it gathered continues teaching us.

Explorer’s glossary

Rover

A robot that moves across a world’s surface to explore different places.

Lander

A spacecraft that lands and studies a place from the surface.

Instrument

A science tool that observes or measures something.

Some instruments discussed here were:

  • Passive Reflectors: It is simply an object that reflects signals, such as lasers or radio waves.
  • Seismometers: A seismometer is a scientific instrument used to detect and measure vibrations or shaking of the ground, such as earthquakes and seismic waves.
  • Laser-ranging retroreflectors: They are special mirrors designed to reflect a laser beam directly back to its source, no matter the angle at which the laser hits them.
  • Antenna: An antenna is a device that sends and receives electromagnetic waves such as radio waves.
Probe

A spacecraft sent to investigate space or another world.

Radio signal

Information carried by radio waves between a spacecraft and Earth.

Data

Information collected by science tools, such as measurements and images.

Orbit

A repeating path around another object, such as a spacecraft circling Mars.

Flyby

A close pass that lets a spacecraft study a world without landing.
